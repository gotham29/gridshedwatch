The problem

Many methods claim to see grid emergencies coming. No common set of real emergencies tests them.

Own events

Each study picks its own storms, often a single one, or simulated grids.

Per-hour metrics

They reward flagging many hours, not one alert in time. Point-adjusted F1 even ranks random scores above every real method.

False alarms unseen

An operator pays for every false alarm, but most evaluations never count them where nothing happened.

Weather services solved this decades ago: score warnings as events, by how many were caught, how early, and with how many false alarms.

Motivation: Winter Storm Uri (2021) cut power to 4.5 million people in Texas; Elliott (2022) forced load sheds at six Southeastern utilities

Supply-shortfall emergencies: an operator declares an Energy Emergency Alert (EEA) and may order firm load shed (rotating outages). NERC defines EEA 3 as firm load interruption imminent or in progress. Weather verification reference: Brooks & Correia (2018) on NWS tornado warnings: probability of detection, false alarm ratio, lead time. Per-hour metric critique: Kim et al. (AAAI 2022) showed point-adjusted F1 rewards random scores; Wu & Keogh (2023) showed anomaly benchmarks are flawed. If asked “why not just use VUS-PR”: it scores hours, and our test shows its ranking barely agrees with events caught.
