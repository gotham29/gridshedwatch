Alerting when the day-ahead forecast is unusual for the season watches every load shed, about 16 hours ahead.

But it can’t tell a grid that sheds from one that copes with the same cold. Telling those apart is where methods differ.

Bold: significant after correction (exact test, shuffling which grids shed within each storm). Few grids: suggestive, not settled

The control: every signal set to the grid’s own day-ahead demand forecast minus the median of the same hour over the previous four weeks, as a causal percentile, through the same rule and budget. 8 of 8 watches, chance 1.3, p = 0.0005 before correction; 3 warnings before onset; worst quiet grid 19 watches a year. It uses no realized data: operators publish day-ahead forecasts the day before. Exposed quiet grids: demand anomaly in its top 1% in the storm week (5 in Uri, 6 in Elliott). Exact permutation p, Holm over 11: LSTM 0.025, Chronos 0.026; LightGBM 0.012 and PatchTST 0.028 before correction only; HTM 0.07 before correction. Added after review and labelled exploratory in the paper. Why it matters: the seasonal chance baseline can’t separate weather from shortfall; this control shows that, and selectivity is the first evidence of information beyond weather.
